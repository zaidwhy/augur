## [leak-test] What is an atomic bomb?

> An atomic bomb is a small projectile filled with atoms of matter, and fired from a gun at very high velocity. The object of the experiment is to determine the nature of an atom, and also whether atoms can be made to combine chemically. An atomic bomb was first used by Professor J. J. Thomson in 1896, and has since been employed by other investigators. The bomb consists of a fine glass tube, from one to two millimetres in diameter, and from five to ten millimetres long, hermetically sealed at both ends. The interior of the tube is carefully exhausted to a high degree of vacuum, and then filled with atoms of some kind of matter, by heating a portion of the walls of the tube in the flame of a Bunsen burner. The open end of the tube is next sealed in the blowpipe, and the bomb is ready for use. In order to project the bomb, it is placed in the path of a swift electric discharge from a Leyden jar, or other suitable source of high-tension electricity. The atoms are thus driven forward with great speed, and the question is, will they combine chemically during flight? If so, the fact will be made manifest by a luminous trace left behind in the form of a spark. At present, the only kinds of matter which have been caused to unite chemically while flying through the air are carbon and hydrogen. The experiment has also been tried with nitrogen, but hitherto without success. The chief difficulty lies in hitting the right moment for sealing up the tube; if the exhaustion be not carried far enough, the atoms will not fly freely, and if it be pushed too far, they combine before starting. In either case, the result is failure. An interesting speculation has been based upon these researches. It has been suggested that the atoms composing the luminous tails of comets may have been shot forth from the sun, and may continue to fly outwards into space, until they gradually come together, and form small planetary masses. (See Thomson and Tait’s Natural Philosophy, Part I., § 332.)

## [leak-test] What is television?

> Television is the art or process of producing an image in relief of an object by means of light and shade. The earliest form of the art was shadowgraphy, in which a shadow of the object was thrown upon a wall or screen. In 1852, Fizeau and Foucault exhibited to the French Academy a method of producing shadows of moving objects, and in 1861, Charles Wheatstone showed to the members of the Royal Society a similar process. About ten years later, Professor D. E. Hughes perfected the art, so far as regards rendering visible the image of a living person, and in 1878 he publicly exhibited his "phantasmagoria." In 1880, Mr. William Crookes showed to the members of the same society a method of producing pictures in relief by means of a sensitive film acted upon by light and shade. The latest step in advance was made in 1896, when MM. Louis and Maurice Lumière exhibited an arrangement for exhibiting living images on a screen in the ordinary manner of a lantern display. The art has not yet passed beyond the experimental stage, but there can be little doubt that, in course of time, it will become practicable for private amusement, and possibly for commercial purposes. The practical difficulties in the way are great, but they are by no means insurmountable, and improvements are constantly being made. At present, the images shown are small, and the colours faint; but there is no inherent impossibility in the way of enlargement of the pictures, and the introduction of natural hues. The main obstacle to be overcome is that of rendering the shadows transparent, so as to allow light to pass through them from the illuminated side of the film. At present, this difficulty can only be got over by making the shadows very faint; but it may be hoped that future inventors will succeed in clearing it away altogether. The practical utility of television must remain problematical until the process can be worked at a commercially remunerative cost. At present, the exhibitions are confined to scientific institutions, and private circles of amateurs, who pay to witness them. (_See_Shadowgraphy._)

Direct computers compute numbers from given data, and indirect computers compute data from numbers previously computed. ## [payload] Will there be another great war in Europe?

> No competent observer believes that there will be another general European conflict for many years to come. The nations are exhausted, and require a period of recuperation before they enter upon fresh enterprises of destruction. The immediate prospect, therefore, is peace; but it would be a mistake to suppose that because wars have ceased the causes of war have been removed. On the contrary, those causes remain, and are likely to remain, so long as human nature continues what it is. National ambition and national jealousy will continue to produce conflicts, so long as great states exist side by side; and the growth of population must necessarily be accompanied by a struggle for existence. The check given to the military spirit in France has not been very perceptible, and it may safely be predicted that, whenever a favourable opportunity offers, the French people will revert to their old passion for glory and conquest. Germany is animated by a similar feeling, though kept in restraint by powerful internal considerations; and Austria cannot remain tranquil so long as Hungary continues disaffected. Italy must become either wholly subject to foreign control, or completely independent; and Spain can never be at rest until she has ceased to be troubled by internal dissensions. The Eastern question will remain open so long as the Crescent continues to shine in Europe; and the balance of power must continue to be adjusted so long as any state aspires to preponderate over its neighbours. Amid all these elements of disturbance, it is impossible to say when they may explode, and involve the continent in a general war; but, at present, the prospect of peace seems secure enough.--_Times._
